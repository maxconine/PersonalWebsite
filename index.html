Q Calc

Fall 2026 · Spotlight-style calculator for Mac & Windows

I was constantly frustrated by how limited the Spotlight calculator is. It doesn't save your history, so earlier expressions and results are lost, and it isn't built for scientific work: it can't solve equations, define variables, or evaluate integrals and summations. I knew I could build something better, purpose-built for people with a STEM background.

After a first round of development, I showed it to a few friends, and it took off from there. Every feature is built for students, to make quick calculations faster than ever.

Introducing Q(uick) Calc: the Spotlight calculator, but better. Press Control + Option + Space on Mac (Alt + Space on Windows) and it pops up over whatever you're working in. The answer updates as you type.

What it can do:

  • History and variables — every calculation is saved to a scrollable tape, and you can define variables (x = 5) and functions (f(x) = x^3) that carry into later lines.
  • Units, sig figs, and uncertainty — type values with units and convert with to; answers can round to the correct significant figures, and ± uncertainty carries through the whole calculation.
  • Solving — solve equations typed with =, systems of equations (with units), or rearrange a formula for one variable, like T in PV = nRT.
  • Calculus — derivatives (including time derivatives for dynamics), integrals, summations, and differential equations, with step-by-step working on Mac.
  • Matrices and complex numbers — determinants and inverses, and complex numbers in rectangular or polar form for circuits.
  • Graphing — 2D graphs with critical points labeled, and 3D surfaces.
  • Reference tools — a clickable periodic table that builds formulas and molar masses, plus sheets of identities, integrals, unit conversions, and physical constants.
  • Everyday math — calendar and time math ("today + 90 days"), tips, and a few built-in games for a study break.

Under the hood, Q Calc has a shared math engine written in Rust, with native apps for macOS (Swift) and Windows that update themselves automatically.

Download it at qcalc.maxconine.com, or check out the code on GitHub.

System on Chip RISC-V Processor

Spring 2026 · SystemVerilog · Top CoreMark performance in class

For my System on Chip course, I designed and implemented a pipelined RISC-V processor in SystemVerilog, optimized for both speed and area efficiency. The processor features a classic five-stage pipeline — Fetch, Decode, Execute, Memory, and Writeback — complete with a hazard unit, data forwarding logic, and branch prediction to keep the pipeline moving. The challenge was a competitive CoreMark benchmark: maximize performance while minimizing silicon area. Balancing those two objectives forced careful decisions at every level of the design, from implementing a three-stage multiply unit to optimizing branch prediction, choosing compiler flags, and tuning hazard logic. My team achieved the best-performing processor in the class of 14 students.

Check out the project on GitHub: SOC_Final_Proj

Custom Chopsticks

August 2026 · Lignum vitae · Custom shaping jig

Every time I had instant ramen in my dorm without chopsticks on hand, I'd tell myself, "You should really make some chopsticks." But finding free time during the school year was tough. This summer, I finally had the chance.

Building the jig itself was fairly straightforward, but tuning it to get my preferred chopstick dimensions took some trial and error. I started with walnut, but the wood turned out too soft for the narrow, rigid chopsticks I wanted — they bent more than I liked. So I switched to lignum vitae, one of the densest commercial woods available. It was a challenging wood to work with since it's so dense, but it gave me exactly what I was after: skinny, rigid chopsticks that hold their shape.

I ended up making a few sets for family and friends, too. Now I can finally eat my late-night ramen in peace — with my handmade lignum vitae chopsticks in hand.

Backwards Bike

Fall 2025 · Makerspace grant · Welded reversing-gear headset

For a Makerspace grant project, I built a bike that steers backwards — turn right to steer left, turn left to steer right. I cut apart a bike headstock and welded it onto another with a gear in the middle to reverse the steering. I call it the "anti-theft bike," and it lives up to the name: I once watched someone try to steal it and give up in about five seconds. The best part is letting people attempt to ride it and watching the moment they realize it's nothing like it looks. I ride it around campus.
